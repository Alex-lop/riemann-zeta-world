Zeta World

Ground: s = σ + it. Height: log(1 + |ζ(s)|). Color: the phase (angle) of ζ(s). The white line is the critical line σ = ½. Green dots are zeros.

The same ground and coloring, now for Riemann's completed function ξ(s). It has exactly the same zeros as ζ in the strip, but its landscape is a mirror image across the white line.

How ξ relates to ζ ξ(s) = ½ · s(s−1) · π−s/2 · Γ(s/2) · ζ(s)

Each extra factor cancels something in ζ:

  • s − 1 cancels the pole of ζ at s = 1 (the spike in the ζ(s) view).
  • Γ(s/2) has poles at s = 0, −2, −4, … They cancel the trivial zeros of ζ at −2, −4, …, and the factor s cancels the pole at 0.
  • π−s/2Γ(s/2) is the factor that turns ζ(s) = χ(s)·ζ(1−s) into a clean symmetry.

What is left has no poles, and its zeros are exactly the nontrivial zeros of ζ. It satisfies

ξ(s) = ξ(1−s)  and  ξ(s̄) = conj ξ(s)

Together: ξ(1−s̄) = conj ξ(s). The point 1−s̄ is the mirror image of s across σ = ½, so |ξ| is mirror-symmetric, and on the line itself ξ is real. That is why the white line shows only two colors: positive and negative, switching at each zero.

Scaling. |ξ(½+it)| shrinks like e−πt/4 (about 10−31 at t = 100), so the terrain plots ξ̃ = ξ(s) / N(t), where N(t) is the size of the extra factors at ½ + it. N(t) is positive and does not depend on σ, so the zeros, the colors and the mirror symmetry are unchanged. Along the white line |ξ̃| = |ζ|, so the ζ(s) and ξ(s) views have the same height there. In fact ξ̃(½ + it) = −Z(t), Hardy's function.

The zeros rebuild the primes. The white staircase is the exact prime count ψ(x). The gold curve is Riemann's explicit formula using only the first N zeros. Add zeros and watch it lock onto the steps.

How the zeros build the primes

Counting primes. ψ(x) adds log p for every prime p ≤ x, and again for p², p³, … It carries the same information as the plain count π(x) but has a cleaner formula. It jumps at every prime (the green ticks).

ψ(x) = x − Σρ xρ/ρ − log 2π − ½ log(1 − x−2)
  • x comes from the pole of ζ at s = 1, the spike in the ζ tab. It is the overall trend.
  • Each zero ρ = ½ + iγ, together with its conjugate, adds a wave −2·Re(xρ/ρ). It oscillates like cos(γ log x) and has size about 2√x/γ. The strip below the chart shows the newest one.
  • log 2π comes from the value of ζ′/ζ at 0, and the last term from the trivial zeros −2, −4, ….

With no zeros the formula is a smooth curve. Each zero adds a higher-frequency wave, and together they carve out the steps, much as a Fourier series builds a square wave (including the small overshoot at each step).

Why the Riemann Hypothesis matters. A zero with real part β contributes a wave of size about xβ. On the critical line β = ½, so every wave grows like √x. Turn on the error view: ψ(x) − x stays on the scale of √x. A single zero off the line would make the error grow faster, and the primes would be less evenly spread.

Dirichlet L-functions generalize ζ: each character χ weights the terms of the series. The ground, colors and probe work as in the ζ tab. The window can now go below t = 0, because for a complex χ the lower half is not a copy of the upper.

χ(a) for a = 1 … q: the color and the hand show the angle; hollow means χ(a) = 0
How L(s, χ) generalizes ζ

The character. A Dirichlet character χ mod q repeats every q steps, is multiplicative (χ(mn) = χ(m)χ(n)), and is 0 on numbers sharing a factor with q. Its values are roots of unity. ζ is the case q = 1, where every weight is 1.

L(s, χ) = Σ χ(n)/nˢ = ∏p 1/(1 − χ(p)p−s)

Characters separate the primes by their remainder mod q. That is how Dirichlet proved that every progression a, a + q, a + 2q, … with gcd(a, q) = 1 contains infinitely many primes.

Completed function. With 𝔞 = 0 for even χ (χ(−1) = 1) and 𝔞 = 1 for odd χ:

Λ(s, χ) = (q/π)(s+𝔞)/2 Γ((s+𝔞)/2) L(s, χ) Λ(s, χ) = ε(χ) · Λ(1 − s, χ̄),  |ε(χ)| = 1

What to look for.

  • Left–right mirror, always. In the Λ view the terrain is mirrored across σ = ½ for every χ: |Λ(s, χ)| = |Λ(1 − s̄, χ)|.
  • Up–down mirror, only for real χ. Since L(σ − it, χ) = conj L(σ + it, χ̄), the terrain below t = 0 is the terrain of χ̄. For a complex χ the zeros sit at different heights above and below the axis; the counts below the controls show it.
  • Trivial zero. In the L view, near the real axis: s = 0 for even χ, s = −1 for odd χ (gray marker). Λ has none, because the Γ factor cancels it.

GRH. The generalized Riemann hypothesis says that for every χ all zeros in the strip lie on σ = ½. A reflected zero of L(s, χ) is a zero of L(s, χ̄), a different function, so a proof has to treat all characters together. (See the README for the proof announced in November 2026.)

Scaling in the Λ view. As for ξ, Λ is divided by its size on the critical line, a positive function of t, and rotated by ε−1/2. Then it is real on the line and |Λ̃| = |L| there, so both views have the same height along the white line.
