How the announced proof works, in four pictures

If a zero of ζ sat off the critical line, the primes would show it: their count would swing too far from its expected value. The announced proof caps those swings from above, using arithmetic. These pictures walk through that strategy one step at a time. One hypothetical zero, which you can move, carries through the steps.

Status. On 20 November 2026 Levent Alpöge announced a proof of the Riemann hypothesis and of its generalization to Dirichlet L-functions, as a formal proof in Lean. It has been machine-checked by independent kernels but not yet refereed, and no human-readable write-up exists. This page follows the account in riemann-rev. The prime data in steps 2 and 3 are computed here and match riemann-rev's figures. Step 4 is a metaphor, not the actual construction.

STEP 1

A zero off the line has a mirror twin

The functional equation pairs every zero at β + iγ with one at 1 − β + iγ, its reflection across the white line (the symmetry you can see in the ξ(s) view of the explorer).

So a zero left of the line forces one to the right of it. To prove the Riemann hypothesis it is therefore enough to show that no zero lies to the right of the line, in the shaded half.

Drag the red point. It is hypothetical: no zero off the line has ever been found. The green points are the actual first zeros, all on the line.

STEP 2

An off-line zero would break a ceiling in the primes

The proof works with a weighted count of primes, G(x). Each zero ρ adds a wave to it, and the wave's size grows like xβ, where β is the zero's real part.

Divided by √x, G must stay inside a fixed band if every zero is on the line: ±0.0462. The gray curve is the actual value, computed from every prime up to 10 million. It stays well inside the band.

Add the hypothetical zero, and its wave grows like xβ − ½. However small it starts, it eventually breaks through the band. Move β toward ½ and the breakthrough moves later, but it never goes away.

Beyond 10⁷ only the hypothetical wave is drawn; the primes there are not computed on this page.

STEP 3

Only the upward swings, and only on some windows

This is the analytic heart of the proof, a "localized Landau theorem". You do not need to show the prime count stays in the band everywhere. It is enough to show it never goes above a ceiling of about √x, on infinitely many long windows from Y to Y3/2.

The reason: an off-line zero's wave swings up as well as down, and grows. Sooner or later it must push above the ceiling inside one of those windows. Weights that are all positive keep the waves of different zeros from cancelling each other out.

So the lower half is grayed out here, and the ceiling is a wall only over the windows.

In the proof the ceiling is x½ + ε for any small ε, and the windows can be chosen as far out as needed. The three windows here are only an illustration.

STEP 4 metaphor

The ceiling comes from divisibility, and the balance gives √x

Where can an upper bound on a prime count come from? From a basic fact: a whole number that is not zero and is divisible by many primes cannot be small. It is at least their product.

The proof builds special numbers out of arithmetic geometry (theta functions and a chain of "theta lifts") that are divisible by many primes, and it also bounds their size from above. Squeezed between the two, only so many primes can divide them. That limit turns into the ceiling of step 3.

The construction is repeated m times. One cost grows like x/m, the other like m. Their sum is smallest at m = √x, and that is where the square root in the Riemann hypothesis comes from.

Both pictures are schematic: they show the shape of the argument, not the actual objects.

Putting it together: the arithmetic of step 4 caps the upward swings (step 3); an off-line zero would break that cap (step 2); so no zero lies right of the line, and by the mirror (step 1) none lies left of it. Running the same argument with Dirichlet characters gives the generalized hypothesis.
