How the announced proof works, in four pictures

If a zero of ζ sat off the critical line, the primes would show it. The announced proof caps the primes from above, using arithmetic. Drag the hypothetical zero on the left and watch its effect on the right. Steps 2 to 4 are tabs.

Status: announced 20 November 2026, machine-checked, not yet refereed Levent Alpöge announced a proof of the Riemann hypothesis and of its generalization to Dirichlet L-functions, as a formal proof in Lean. It has been machine-checked by independent kernels, but it has not been refereed, and no human-readable write-up exists. This page follows the account in riemann-rev. The prime data in the chart are computed here and match riemann-rev's figures. Step 4 is a metaphor, not the actual construction.
STEP 1 The mirror

A zero off the line has a mirror twin. So it is enough to show that no zero lies right of the line, in the shaded half. Drag the red point; the green points are the actual first zeros.

The functional equation pairs every zero at β + iγ with one at 1 − β + iγ, its reflection across the white line (the symmetry you can see in the ξ(s) view of the explorer).

So a zero left of the line forces one to the right of it. To prove the Riemann hypothesis it is therefore enough to show that no zero lies to the right of the line.

The red point is hypothetical: no zero off the line has ever been found.

An off-line zero would break a ceiling in the primes. Divided by √x, the prime sum must stay inside the gold band if every zero is on the line, and the actual primes do. The hypothetical zero's wave grows like xβ − ½ and eventually breaks out.

The proof works with a weighted count of primes, G(x). Each zero ρ adds a wave to it, and the wave's size grows like xβ, where β is the zero's real part. Divided by √x, G must stay inside a fixed band, ±0.0462, if every zero is on the line. The gray curve is the actual value, computed from every prime up to 10 million; it stays well inside. The hypothetical zero's wave grows like xβ − ½: however small it starts, it eventually breaks through. Move β toward ½ and the breakthrough moves later, but it never goes away.

Beyond 10⁷ only the hypothetical wave is drawn; the primes there are not computed on this page.

Only the upward swings, and only on some windows. The proof needs just one side: no swing above a ceiling of about √x on infinitely many long windows [Y, Y3/2]. A growing off-line wave must push through one of them.

This is the analytic heart of the proof, a "localized Landau theorem". You do not need to show the prime count stays in the band everywhere. It is enough to show it never goes above a ceiling of about √x, on infinitely many long windows from Y to Y3/2. An off-line zero's wave swings up as well as down, and grows, so sooner or later it must push above the ceiling inside one of those windows. Weights that are all positive keep the waves of different zeros from cancelling each other out.

In the proof the ceiling is x½ + ε for any small ε, and the windows can be chosen as far out as needed; the three windows here are only an illustration.

The ceiling comes from divisibility, and the balance gives √x. A whole number that is not zero and is divisible by many primes is at least their product. Weighing that against an upper estimate of its size caps the prime count, and balancing two costs, x/m and m, puts the cap at √x.

Where can an upper bound on a prime count come from? From a basic fact: a whole number that is not zero and is divisible by many primes cannot be small. It is at least their product.

The proof builds special numbers out of arithmetic geometry (theta functions and a chain of "theta lifts") that are divisible by many primes, and it also bounds their size from above. Squeezed between the two, only so many primes can divide them. That limit turns into the ceiling of step 3.

The construction is repeated m times. One cost grows like x/m, the other like m. Their sum is smallest at m = √x, and that is where the square root in the Riemann hypothesis comes from.

Both pictures are schematic: they show the shape of the argument, not the actual objects.

Putting it together: the arithmetic of step 4 caps the upward swings (step 3); an off-line zero would break that cap (step 2); so no zero lies right of the line, and by the mirror (step 1) none lies left of it. Running the same argument with Dirichlet characters gives the generalized hypothesis.
